# Storytelling as a shared communication capability

> This is a **shared reference**, not a task-routing Skill. It governs how verified or appropriately qualified knowledge is communicated. The owning Skill decides what research, teaching, or verification must happen first.

## Core principle

An effective explanation helps readers experience a **change of understanding**: known situation → meaningful question → exploration of plausible possibilities → evidence or mechanism → supported conclusion and limitations.

This is an optional narrative scaffold, not a mandatory five-section template, historical reenactment, or fiction.

## Audience and presentation

1. Identify what the reader already knows and which question is still unanswered. Do not re-teach familiar material merely because it fits an introductory story.
2. Start with a concrete, relevant observation or question, not a glossary. For a specialist reader, lead with the substantive delta, evidence, or unresolved trade-off.
3. Maintain a continuous causal thread: each section should answer an outstanding question or add information needed for the next step.
4. Use one stable example where appropriate; show input → operation → observable output. Introduce a term only after its job is intelligible.
5. Treat counterexamples as real discoveries. A plausible simpler alternative can work; acknowledge it before explaining the engineering trade-off. Never fabricate a failure to create a dramatic turn.
6. Keep fictional situations, toy numbers, metaphors, source quotations, measured results and independent inferences distinct.
7. Include the qualification that materially changes the conclusion, even if it interrupts the story. Reduce rhetorical questions, hero narratives, artificial surprises, and emotional flourishes.
8. Match format and length to the task. Short questions may need two or three direct sentences; research findings may need a comparison table first; legal, medical and financial checks prioritize explicit evidence and risks.

## Delivery modes

- **Teaching:** scenario/puzzle → reader's reasonable hypothesis → mechanism → minimal verification → limits. The goal is transfer of understanding.
- **Research findings:** conclusion and evidence boundaries first → comparison or competing explanations → what the evidence actually supports → implications. A story is optional, never a substitute for independent source analysis.
- **Reflection / nonfiction:** observed event → original interpretation → evidence that changed the view → revised understanding; never invent the user's experience.
- **Revision:** restructure missing causal steps before polishing wording.

## Fast presentation check

- What exactly can a reader explain or decide after reading that they could not before?
- Are all pivotal claims anchored to checked facts, clear hypotheses, or expressly labeled illustrative examples?
- Does removing the metaphor leave the factual argument intact?
- Could a reader distinguish the source author's conclusion from the assistant's interpretation?
- Is the format helping the task, rather than forcing a literary shape?
